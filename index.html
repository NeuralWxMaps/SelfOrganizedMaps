Self-Organized Maps

Meteorologists have long recognized that similar weather patterns can produce similar types of weather on a regional scale, especially during more significant weather events. But quantifying the similarity of weather patterns has always been a challenge. Collecting a number of case studies related to a particular set of weather events, like Atlantic Bomb Cyclogenesis, Pacific Atmospheric River Rainfall episodes, or Four Corner Upper Level Lows, shows the similarities that meteorologists focus on when forecasting future extreme weather events. But a collection of case studies gives little indication of how often a certain pattern has developed, or what its climatological probability might be. An organized catalog, or atlas, of historical weather patterns does not seem to exist, other than the chronological record in datasets like the NCAR Reanalysis. This project will set out to propose a possible catalog of weather patterns, and look into some possible uses for such a weather pattern catalog.

My proposal is to use a partial implementation of Self-Organized Maps, intoduced by Teuvo Kohonen in the late 1980s. Using the idea that a weather map can be represented as an array of grid points across a selected domain, the arrays in a training dataset can be compared to a neural network of arrays, whose size is equal to the number of grid points in the domain.

A simplified example can show how the training of a Self-Organized Map works. Start with a 5-by-5 pattern(array) and a neural network of 25 arrays, initialized with values of zero.

Calculate the sum of differences squared between training array and the arrays of each node of the neural network, looking for the node that has the lowest value. The node in the neural network with the lowest value will be chosen as the winning node. In this case, all of the differences are equal, so node 0 is chosen as the winning node.

Next, the winning node of the neural network and its adjacent neighbors are adjusted toward the training pattern according to some simple rules. In this example, the winning node is adjusted half way toward the training array. The directly adjacent nodes are adjusted one-quarter of the way toward the training array, and the diagonally adjacent node is adjusted one-eighth of the way toward the training array. The adjusted network becomes the neural network that will be used in the next iteration of the algorithm.

For the next iteration, calculate differences between a new pattern and each of the nodes in the neural network, select the winning node, and then adjust the network based on the rules.

One more iteration will show a node other than Node 0 being the winning node, allowing the neural network(self-organized map) to begin expanding.

As more patterns are cycled through the algorithm, more and more nodes in the self-organized map will be defined, and unique patterns will emerge. Since only neighboring nodes are affected on successive iterations, much of the neural network remains unchanged each time, while local adjustments are applied.

Using the 2.5 degree by 2.5 degree dataset in the NCAR Reanalysis, I chose a domain covering the northeast Pacific Ocean and almost all of North America. This domain was a 23-by-45 grid, meaning that the Self-Organized Map, or Neural Network, would have 1035 points, or solenoids.

With the 23-by-45 neural network established, a training dataset stretching from 1951 through 2025 was passed through the algorithm. After identifying the closest pattern, the matching solenoid and 48 of the surrounding solenoids were adjusted toward the latest training array, with the adjustment diminishing as the euclidean distance from the matching solenoid increased. In the first epoch of training, I used every 9th time period of the year from the years 1951, 1954, 1957...2020, 2023. In the second epoch, every 7th time period of the year was used from the years 1952, 1955, 1958...2021, 2024. And the third epoch involved every 5th time period of the year from the years 1953, 1956, 1959...2022, 2025. In this manner, 16,500 unique weather patterns, spanning 75 years were run through the training algorithm to define the 1035 nodes of the neural network.

The first parameter that I worked with was the 500 hectoPascal(hPa) geopotential height field. There is a seasonal variation in geopotential height fields with higher values in the summer and lower values in the winter. In order to remove this seasonal variation, the height value of the center grid point of the training weather pattern was subtracted from the rest of the array as it was run through the algorithm.

The resulting self-organized map of weather patterns is a collection of patterns with neighboring nodes being similar, but not identical, to each other. Moving across the rows and columns of the neural network of weather patterns shows increasing variety as euclidean distance increases. After all of the solenoids in the neural network have been defined, it is possible to assign any weather pattern to a single node of the neural network. The third meterological parameter that I generated a Self-Organized Map for was observed precipitation over the western United States. Processing this data involved a different grid domain and a different dataset. The National Weather SErvice produces a very-high resolution analysis model that covers the continental United States called the Real Time Mesoscale Analysis(RTMA). This model produces data at grid resolution finer than 1.0 degree by 1.0 degree and temporal resolution at and below 1 hour. For my study I collected hourly data across the western and central United States at 0.5 degree by 0.5 degree areal resolution. The domain stretched from 24.0 degrees north latitude to 50.5 degrees north latitude and from 125.0 degrees west longitude to 95.5 degrees west longitude. The hourly data was summed up into six-hour time blocks to match the temporal resolution of upper air meteorological parameters collected from NCAR's Reanalysis dataset. The selected domain gave a 53 by 59 grid which means the Self-Organized Map would have 3,127 nodes, or unique precipitation patterns. Beginning in October 2021 and continuing through the end of 2024, the 6-hourly precipitation data was run through the the SOM training algorithm. This represents around 4,700 patterns, which is only marginally more than the patterns that are trying to be defined. Data for 2025 and 2026 are available to be run through the training algorthm which would refine pattern definition within the nodes, but for now the three-plus years of data appears sufficient to post some initial findings.
